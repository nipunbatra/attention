Keep the ViT encoder. Change how we train it.

The image supplies a training target

In Part I, we turned an image into patch rows and passed them through a Transformer. A classifier can learn from those rows when we have labelled images. But a large image collection may have no class labels. Self-supervised learning constructs a training target from each image itself: hidden pixels, a teacher's output on another view, or a representation of a hidden region. These are different objectives, even when they use a similar ViT encoder.

This is the $4\times4$ grayscale image from Part I, split into four $2\times2$ patches.

We have its pixel values, but no class label. What could the model learn to predict?

Hide three patches from the encoder. Keep their original pixels as the target.

The model must predict what it cannot read. Across many images, that task can train useful visual representations.

$r_j$: the four raw pixels in patch $j$.

$\ve{e_j}=r_jW_{\mathrm{patch}}+b_{\mathrm{patch}}+p_j$: the input representation for that patch, as in Part I.

$E$ stacks the representation rows. We reserve $v,V$ for attention values, as before.

The drawing uses four patches so we can see every input. Real pretraining uses many images and usually many more patches. Its objective does not by itself prove that the representation will work for recognition, segmentation, or another downstream task; that requires evaluation.

We will compare three target choices: MAE reconstructs pixels, DINO matches a teacher's probability distribution across views, and I-JEPA predicts target-region features. Our browser exercises calculate losses from disclosed inputs. They do not run a pretrained model.

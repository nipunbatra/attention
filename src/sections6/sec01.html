Vision I needed a label for every image. Who writes millions of labels?

Labels are expensive; images are free

In Vision I an encoder learned from two labelled scenes. A label is a fact about an image that a person wrote down, and people are slow. Images, on the other hand, arrive by the billion without anyone writing anything. This part asks what an encoder can learn from an image alone. The answer is the one trick behind MAE, DINO and I-JEPA: hide or transform part of the image and ask the model for what the image already contains. We keep the same 8×8 scene and the frozen encoder from Vision I, so every number in the three methods can be traced on the page.

142 million images, zero labels.

ImageNet's labels took about 49,000 crowd workers in 167 countries. DINOv2 learned from ten times as many images that nobody labelled. The bars are sourced numbers, not toy numbers: ImageNet (Deng et al. 2009), MAE (arXiv 2111.06377), DINOv2 (arXiv 2304.07193).

Hide part of scene A and ask for it back. The hidden pixels are the label; the image supplied them itself.

Three targets from the image alone: the hidden pixels (MAE), a teacher's answer on another view (DINO), the hidden patch's feature (I-JEPA).
  • 1 scene, 4 hidden patches, 3 or 4 training images
  • axes named because $W_{\mathrm{patch}}$ is fixed
  • every number from train_vision2.py; nothing about real photographs
  • The encoder is Vision I's, frozen. Vision I trained it with labels to answer "is there a mug on the right?". Real MAE, DINO and I-JEPA train the whole encoder; here only small parts on top of it learn: a 32-number decoder, a 22-number predictor, a 15-number head and a 3-number probe.
  • One scene, four hidden patches and three or four training images are enough to trace every number and too few to learn a general skill. Section 8 reports a failure as well as a success.
  • The axis names (brightness, contrast, row, col) are true because Vision I fixed the patch projection. Trained encoders do not name their axes.
  • DINO's centre, temperatures and moving average are the real mechanisms at toy settings (temperatures 1 and 0.5, momentum 0.9), and the two collapses they prevent are shown by switching them off.
  • Every number is computed on this page from the parameters that train_vision2.py produced with a fixed seed. The losses are exact for this toy and say nothing about accuracy on real photographs.

With that said once, the sections can concentrate on the mechanisms. Keep one question in mind for each method: where does the target come from, and what could the model learn to predict it? Section 2 starts with the simplest target of all, the hidden pixels.

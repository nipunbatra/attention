What a trained model attends to

Attention runs for all seventeen rows at once, not only for CLS. Each patch row forms its own query, reads the other rows and receives its own update, which a second layer would use. Pick any receiver and any scene and the overlay shows its row of $\va{A}$. In this one-head toy the picture repeats itself, and the numbers say why: the trained query depends on brightness alone, so every row asks the same question at a different volume, and a dark patch asks nothing and reads every row equally. Real Vision Transformers have several heads and many layers; the last frame links to their published maps instead of drawing one we did not compute.

The overlay is a row of $\va{A}$ painted on the input, and the same picture is drawn for real models: which patches does CLS read in the last layer, head by head?

Dosovitskiy et al. (An image is worth 16×16 words, 2021, figures 6 and 7) combine the maps of all layers by attention rollout and find that the highlighted patches often cover the object named by the label, with some heads reading globally already in the first layers.

Caron et al. (Emerging properties in self-supervised Vision Transformers, DINO, 2021) show CLS maps of a self-supervised ViT whose heads follow object outlines without any label.

A map shows where one row read on one forward pass. It does not by itself show that those patches caused the answer; our value-only intervention in section 6 is the kind of test that question needs.

Every row reads every row, and in our toy they all ask the same question at different volumes. The overlay is the picture to carry to the next parts: what a row reads is what its update is made of.

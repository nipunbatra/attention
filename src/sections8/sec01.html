Vision III could pick the closest caption from a list. Now we want the model to write the answer itself.

CLIP can rank captions; it cannot write an answer

What this toy can and cannot show. It can show every number: the frozen encoder rows, who may read whom, what the question mark reads, how the answer changes between two scenes, one probe scene, and one training step. It cannot show general counting, natural photographs, or questions outside its ten-word vocabulary. The two fitted scenes prove image dependence, not understanding; the probe in Section 7 shows where that ends.

Part 2 opened with a sentence and a blank. This part opens with a picture and a blank. The four words how many mugs ? are the same for every picture, so whatever fills the blank must come from the image. CLIP, from Vision III, can only compare the picture with captions we write for it. Here a language decoder, the same one-head block as Part 2, reads sixteen patch rows and the question, and predicts the next token. Switch the scene under the bars: the answer moves with the mugs, before you see any machinery.

Vision III: CLIP

  • input: the image and a list of captions
  • output: one score per caption

Best match: two mugs on a table. Asked how many mugs?, it has nothing to rank.

Vision IV: an image-conditioned decoder

  • input: the image rows and the question
  • output: a probability for every next token

It writes: two, then a stop marker.

A question needs next-token prediction. The image joins the context, exactly like the earlier words in Part 2.

The question is a handful of tokens. The image is hundreds of rows in front of it, and each row will offer a key and a value.

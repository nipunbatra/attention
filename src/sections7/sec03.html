Encode and normalise

The image side reuses Vision I. Its trained encoder reads the 16 patches through CLS and writes an updated CLS row with four coordinates: brightness, contrast, row and col. We freeze that encoder and add one learned map from those four numbers to the three joint axes. The text side is smaller still: add the rows of the words in the caption. Both sides then divide by their own length, so an image and a caption are compared by direction alone. Six unit vectors result, and the sphere picture at the end shows them all. The captions start exactly on the axes; the images start near their own axis, because we chose the starting map that way.

What CLS reads, and the row it carries out

The encoder is frozen. Its updated CLS row is all the image side hands on.

Look at the three carried-out rows: they lie on one line. Vision I's encoder was trained to answer one question, so its update is one message scaled by how much bright material CLS read. That is why one number, essentially the row coordinate, orders the scenes: plant, book, mugs. A single linear map cannot make three points on a line into three orthogonal axes, but it can tilt the line so that each scene's largest coordinate is its own axis. That is what the starting map below does.

$\ve{g^{\mathrm{img}}} = e_0^{\prime}\,W_{\mathrm{img}}$: four CLS coordinates in, three named coordinates out

cls_row = frozen_encoder(scene)[0]   # [4], no gradient
g_img = cls_row @ W_img              # [3], learned map

One line per output axis: the CLS row times one column of $W_{\mathrm{img}}$

$\vq{g^{\mathrm{txt}}}$ for “”: add the row of every word

counts = word_counts(caption)   # [14]
g_txt = counts @ W_txt          # [3], the rows added up

$$\ve{\hat g^{\mathrm{img}}} = \frac{\ve{g^{\mathrm{img}}}}{\lVert \ve{g^{\mathrm{img}}} \rVert}, \qquad \vq{\hat g^{\mathrm{txt}}} = \frac{\vq{g^{\mathrm{txt}}}}{\lVert \vq{g^{\mathrm{txt}}} \rVert}$$

g_img = F.normalize(g_img, dim=-1)
g_txt = F.normalize(g_txt, dim=-1)

A classifier can only say the names on its menu. A caption can say anything.

A fixed menu or an open caption

What this toy can and cannot show

  • The image encoder is Vision I's trained encoder on the shared 8×8 scene, kept frozen. It was trained for one yes-or-no question, so it summarises a scene by how much bright material it read. Only the small map after it learns here.
  • The text encoder adds one learned row per word from a 14-word vocabulary. Word order is ignored.
  • Training uses three pairs for 60 steps. That is memorisation of three examples; pairs 4 to 8 and the extra prompts are probes, not a test set.
  • A softmax over a candidate list compares that list and nothing else. It is not the probability that a caption is true.
  • Real CLIP trains both encoders from scratch on hundreds of millions of pairs. Every number on this page comes from the toy; claims about the full model cite the paper.

Vision I ended with a classifier: a head with one row per class name. Ask it about a scene it was not built for and it still answers from its menu. Below, the book-only scene is pushed through that frozen head and must choose between mug on the right and no mug on the right. Neither is a description of a book. A caption has no such limit. Someone who uploads a photo writes a sentence next to it, and that sentence can name any object, any count, any colour. CLIP's idea is to learn from those sentences: pair every image with its caption and learn to tell the true pairings from the wrong ones.

A caption can name any object, count or sense. If a model can match images with captions, the caption list becomes the classifier.

1,000class names in the usual ImageNet menu. A new object needs a new head and new labels.

400 millionimage and caption pairs collected from the web to train CLIP (Radford and colleagues, 2021). The captions were already written.

Captions are supervision that comes with the image. The learning problem: recognise which caption belongs to which image.

The image side is Vision I's encoder, frozen. Everything to its right is new, and it is small: a 4×3 map, one row per word, and one temperature.

Two encoders end in the same three named coordinates: mug, book, plant. Comparing an image with a caption is one dot product.

A classifier chooses from a fixed menu; a caption is open text. CLIP learns to score an image against any caption, so the candidate list can be written at the time of asking.

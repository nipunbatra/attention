Compute the vectors before comparing them.

Pixels and words become comparable vectors

These two small encoders let us calculate both sides of a match. They start from the matrices shown here, not a saved MAE or DINO checkpoint. We carry over the idea of a visual representation and give it a new job: matching a caption.

Flatten the image in row-major order. Multiplying that sixteen-number row by $W_{\mathrm{img}}\in\mathbb R^{16\times3}$ produces one three-number image vector. For text, count the occurrences of each word and multiply by $W_{\mathrm{txt}}\in\mathbb R^{11\times3}$. The initial weights are chosen for readable arithmetic. Training can change every entry.

$\ve{g_1^{\mathrm{img}}}=\underbrace{\operatorname{flatten}(I_1)}_{1\times16}\underbrace{W_{\mathrm{img}}}_{16\times3}=\ve{(2,1,0)}$

These are the only nonzero starting rows. The anchor has $(1,0,0)$ at pixels 1, 3, and 7.

pixels = images.flatten(start_dim=1)  # [3, 16]
g_img = pixels @ W_img                # [3, 3]

$\vq{g_1^{\mathrm{txt}}}=(1,0,0)+(0,0,0)+(1,0,0)=\vq{(2,0,0)}$

counts = word_counts(captions)  # [3, 11], toy vocabulary
g_txt = counts @ W_txt          # [3, 3]

This toy ignores word order. Real CLIP's text Transformer uses sequence information.

$\ve{\hat g_1^{\mathrm{img}}}=\dfrac{\ve{(2,1,0)}}{\sqrt{2^2+1^2}}\approx\ve{(0.894,0.447,0)}$

$\vq{\hat g_1^{\mathrm{txt}}}=\dfrac{\vq{(2,0,0)}}{2}=\vq{(1,0,0)}$

g_img_unit = F.normalize(g_img, dim=-1)
g_txt_unit = F.normalize(g_txt, dim=-1)

Both lengths are now one. Their dot product measures cosine similarity rather than the size of either vector.

Every row is unit length at full precision. Three-decimal displays may not have a norm of exactly one.

The complete starting maps

The encoders output a whole-image or whole-caption vector. A real model has many internal token embeddings and attention values before this global output. Shared output width does not imply that the two encoder architectures, internal widths, or parameters are the same.
